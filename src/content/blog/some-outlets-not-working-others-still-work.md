When several outlets stop working but the rest of the house still has power, the pattern can offer useful clues. The cause might be a tripped GFCI receptacle, a wall switch, a circuit interruption, or a failed connection that requires professional diagnosis.

You can make a few safe observations before calling an electrician. Do not remove an outlet cover, open the electrical panel, touch wiring, or use improvised testing methods. If anything is hot, wet, smoking, sparking, visibly damaged, or has shocked someone, stop troubleshooting and get help immediately.

## First, determine exactly what lost power

Start by identifying the boundaries of the outage. This is safer and more useful than repeatedly trying devices or assuming every dead outlet has the same problem.

Make a short list of what is and is not working:

- Check nearby lights using their normal wall switches.
- Note every affected outlet without removing its cover.
- Look in the rooms on the opposite side of the affected wall.
- Check nearby hallways, bathrooms, the garage, basement, and exterior outlets.
- Note whether a built-in appliance or exhaust fan also stopped working.
- Write down what was running immediately before the power went out.

Use a small, known-working device such as a lamp only if the outlet looks normal and is completely dry. Do not plug anything into a receptacle that is cracked, loose, scorched, melted, buzzing, or discolored. Keep people away from damaged energized equipment until an electrician can assess it.

The pattern matters. One dead receptacle may indicate a problem at that location, while several dead receptacles can point to a shared protective device or an interruption elsewhere on the circuit. An electrician can use your notes to begin tracing the problem more efficiently.

## Look for a GFCI receptacle in a nearby area

A GFCI receptacle usually has “test” and “reset” buttons on its face. One GFCI can protect other outlets located downstream, so the receptacle controlling the outage may not be in the room where you first noticed the problem.

Without removing any covers, look for GFCI receptacles in places such as:

- Bathrooms
- Kitchens
- Garages
- Basements
- Laundry areas
- Exterior walls
- Utility or mechanical spaces

If the GFCI is dry, undamaged, cool, and not making noise, follow the labeling on its face and press the reset button once. A reset that restores power may explain the outage, but pay attention to what happened before it tripped. If it trips again, will not reset, feels warm, or makes a buzzing sound, leave it alone and schedule electrical service.

Never force or hold a reset button in place. Do not keep resetting a device that immediately trips. Repeated operation can obscure an active fault rather than solve it.

Water changes the situation completely. If a receptacle, appliance, fixture, or electrical panel is wet—or if you would have to stand in a wet area to reach it—do not touch it. Keep others away and seek professional help.

## Rule out an ordinary switched outlet

Some wall outlets are controlled by a switch. This arrangement is common where a room was designed for a switched lamp instead of a ceiling fixture. Sometimes only one half of a duplex receptacle is switched.

Before assuming the outlet has failed, operate the room’s ordinary wall switches and check whether the lamp comes on. Do not use a switch that is cracked, loose, hot, buzzing, or scorched.

Also consider recent changes. A switch may have been turned off while moving furniture, cleaning, decorating, or preparing a room for guests. If a switched outlet explains the outage and everything operates normally, no repair may be needed.

However, a switch does not explain outlets that failed suddenly while they were in use, intermittent power, flickering, heat, or a burning odor. Those symptoms deserve professional troubleshooting through [residential electrical service](/residential/).

## Observe the breaker panel without opening it

You may visually inspect the front of the electrical panel as long as the area is dry and the panel appears undamaged. Do not remove the panel cover, reach inside, touch wiring, or attempt to tighten anything.

Look only at the breaker handles and circuit labels. A tripped breaker may rest between its normal on and off positions. If you are uncertain which breaker is involved, or the labeling is unclear, leave it for an electrician.

Do not touch the panel if it is:

- Hot or giving off a burning odor
- Buzzing, crackling, or sparking
- Wet or surrounded by water
- Rusted through or physically damaged
- Missing parts or exposing energized components

If the panel looks normal and you choose to operate a breaker, turn off or unplug devices on the affected circuit first. Follow the breaker’s normal operating motion and try it only once. If it trips again, will not stay on, or the problem returns, leave it off and call an electrician. ## Know when a dead outlet is an urgent warning

A simple loss of power is not always an emergency, but certain accompanying symptoms require immediate action. Stop checking outlets and seek professional or emergency help if you notice:

- Smoke, flames, or a burning smell
- Sparking or crackling
- Heat at an outlet, switch, plug, cord, or panel
- Scorching, melting, or fresh discoloration
- A shock or tingling sensation
- Water in or around electrical equipment
- Damaged equipment that may still be energized

Call 911 if there is fire, visible smoke, or an immediate threat to people or the building. If someone has received an electrical shock, seek emergency medical help rather than continuing to investigate the circuit.

Do not touch a smoking or sparking device to unplug it. Do not pour water on electrical equipment. Keep a safe distance from damaged energized equipment and direct other people away from the area. ## Prepare useful details for the electrician

Good notes can shorten the initial fact-finding conversation without requiring you to perform electrical work. Before calling, write down:

1. Which outlets, lights, and appliances stopped working.
2. Whether the failure happened all at once or intermittently.
3. What equipment was running at the time.
4. Whether a GFCI or breaker tripped.
5. Whether one reset restored power and, if so, for how long.
6. Any sounds, odors, heat, flickering, or visible damage.
7. Whether there has been a leak, spill, or other water exposure.
8. Any recent fixture, appliance, remodeling, or furniture changes near the affected area.

Leave affected outlets accessible, but do not pull them from the wall. Move ordinary household items only when doing so does not require touching damaged equipment. Keep pets and children away from the area, and avoid running extension cords through doorways or across walking paths as a temporary workaround.

## When to schedule troubleshooting

Schedule an electrician when the affected outlets remain dead after basic external checks, a GFCI will not reset, power comes and goes, or the outage has no clear explanation. Professional diagnosis is also appropriate when an outlet is physically loose, plugs fall out easily, or the problem returns after appearing to resolve itself.
