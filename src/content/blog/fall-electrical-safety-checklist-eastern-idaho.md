Cold weather changes how a home uses electricity. Lights stay on longer, portable heaters come out of storage, holiday decorations add cords and plugs, and snow can hide damage around outdoor receptacles and fixtures. A small problem that was easy to ignore in summer can become much less convenient on a dark January evening.

For homeowners in Idaho Falls, Ammon, Ucon, Iona, Shelley, Rigby, Ririe, Roberts, and the surrounding area, early fall is a good time for a simple electrical walk-through. You do not need to remove a panel cover or test live wiring. The useful work is noticing damage, testing only the devices designed for homeowner testing, and scheduling repairs before cold weather increases the load.

## Start with the outlets that will carry winter loads

Think about where portable heaters, humidifiers, heat tape, holiday lights, and other seasonal equipment were used last winter. Inspect those receptacles before plugging anything in.

Stop and schedule a repair if an outlet is cracked, loose in the wall, discolored, or unable to hold a plug firmly. The same applies if a plug or faceplate becomes warm, buzzes, sparks, or gives off a sharp electrical smell. Do not keep using a questionable outlet to see whether the problem clears up.

A receptacle that only works when a plug is held at an angle is not a minor annoyance. It can indicate worn contacts or a poor connection. Replacing or repairing the device is normal [residential electrical work](/residential/); opening the box and working on its wiring is not a safe troubleshooting step for an untrained homeowner.

## Give space heaters their own safety check

Take each portable heater out before you need it. Look at the full length of its cord and at both blades of the plug. A cracked jacket, crushed section, loose blade, darkened plug, or damaged heater housing means the unit should stay unplugged.

The [U.S. Fire Administration's space-heater guidance](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/gallery/pictographs/pictograph41.html) says to plug a space heater directly into a wall outlet, never an extension cord or power strip. Place it on a solid, flat surface and follow the clearances and operating instructions from its manufacturer. Do not run the cord under a rug or through a doorway, and do not leave the heater where it can be knocked over.

If using a heater causes a breaker to trip, do not solve the problem with a power strip, a longer cord, or a larger breaker. Unplug the heater and review our guide to [why a breaker keeps tripping](/blog/why-does-my-breaker-keep-tripping/). Repeated trips under an ordinary load deserve diagnosis, especially if the breaker will not reset or anything feels hot.

## Test GFCI receptacles without taking them apart

Ground-fault circuit interrupters are commonly found where electricity and moisture may meet, including bathrooms, kitchens, garages, basements, and outdoors. They have **TEST** and **RESET** buttons so users can verify the protective function without opening the device.

Follow the instructions printed on the receptacle or supplied by its manufacturer. In general, pressing **TEST** should trip the device and cut power; pressing **RESET** should restore it. If it does not trip, will not reset, feels loose, or shows damage, stop using it and arrange a repair. Do not bypass it or replace it with a standard receptacle.

One GFCI can also protect other receptacles downstream. If several outlets go dead at once while the breaker remains on, check the nearby GFCI devices. If none is tripped—or one will not reset—leave the diagnosis to an electrician instead of opening boxes at random.

## Walk the exterior before snow covers it

Inspect outdoor electrical equipment from the ground in daylight. Look for broken fixture lenses, loose covers, cracked receptacle covers, cords pinched by doors, and equipment that has pulled away from the wall. Water inside a lens or enclosure is a reason to stop using it and schedule service.

Then make a second walk after dusk. Confirm that entry, yard, garage, and security lights operate normally. Note lights that flicker, cycle on and off, or fail together. The pattern can help an electrician separate a failed lamp or driver from a photocell, control, or circuit problem.

Use only cords marked for outdoor use when temporary outdoor power is necessary. The [U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/07_Electrical%20Safety_04.pdf) also advises replacing worn or damaged extension cords and keeping cord-connected equipment away from water. Extension cords are temporary equipment, not permanent wiring for a garage, shed, heat cable, or holiday display.

## Make the electrical panel easy to reach

You can inspect useful things without opening the panel. Clear away boxes, coats, tools, and storage so the door and the area in front of it are accessible. Check that circuit labels are readable and specific enough to help during an outage.

With the panel closed, watch for rust, water marks, scorching, unusual sounds, a burning odor, or obvious damage. Do not remove the cover or tighten components. A panel that is hot, buzzing, sparking, or wet needs prompt professional attention, and you should not approach it if doing so requires standing in water.

Also note any breaker that tripped during normal use last winter. Write down what was running and what part of the home lost power. A clear history is more valuable than repeatedly resetting the breaker and hoping the pattern disappears.

## Check smoke and carbon monoxide alarms

Electrical preparation is only part of cold-weather safety. Follow each alarm manufacturer's testing, battery, placement, and replacement instructions. If a hardwired alarm chirps or fails its test, do not disconnect it and leave the ceiling wiring exposed. Arrange replacement or diagnosis.

Carbon monoxide alarms are especially important around fuel-burning appliances and portable generators. The [CPSC recommends](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center) alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas. If an alarm sounds or anyone may be experiencing carbon monoxide symptoms, move outside to fresh air and call 911.

## Plan backup power before an outage

A portable generator must stay outside, at least 20 feet from the home, with its exhaust facing away, according to the CPSC. Never operate one in a house, garage, basement, crawlspace, or shed—even with doors or windows open.

Never try to power house wiring by plugging a generator into a wall outlet. This practice, called backfeeding, can expose utility workers and neighbors to dangerous voltage and bypass household protection. If you want a generator to supply selected home circuits, have appropriate transfer equipment planned and installed by qualified professionals before the outage. Follow the generator manufacturer's instructions for placement, weather protection, cords, loading, fueling, and shutdown.

## Know which warning signs should not wait

Schedule ordinary repairs before winter when you find a failed light, loose receptacle, unreliable switch, damaged cover, or recurring nuisance trip. Treat these signs as urgent:

- smoke, sparks, or scorch marks;
- a burning or sharp electrical odor;
- a hot panel, switch, receptacle, plug, or cord;
- buzzing or crackling from fixed wiring;
- electrical equipment that is wet or has been submerged;
- a breaker that trips immediately after one reset;
- a shock or tingling sensation from an appliance or metal surface.

If there is active fire, smoke in a wall, or immediate danger, get to a safe location and call 911. Do not touch wet electrical equipment, and do not reach for a breaker when heat, arcing, or standing water makes the approach unsafe.

## Turn the walk-through into a short repair list

Finish with three groups: items to stop using now, repairs to schedule, and improvements to plan. Include the room or fixture, what happened, when it happened, and what else was operating. Photos taken from a safe distance can help, but do not remove covers to get one.
