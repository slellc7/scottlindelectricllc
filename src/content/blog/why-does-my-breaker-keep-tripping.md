A tripping breaker is not a faulty breaker. It is a breaker doing exactly what it was
installed to do: cutting power before a wire gets hot enough to start a fire. The useful
question is never "how do I stop it tripping" — it is "what is it protecting me from."

There are three answers, and you can usually narrow it to one before anybody comes out.

## It trips when you run several things at once

This is an overloaded circuit, and it is the most common cause by a wide margin. A 15-amp
circuit gives you about 1,800 watts. A microwave and a kettle together will clear that on
their own, and if that circuit also feeds the toaster, you have found your answer.

The tell is repeatability: it trips when a specific combination of things is running, it
resets cleanly, and it holds until you do the same thing again.

**What to do:** move one appliance to a different circuit — and check it really is a
different circuit, not another outlet on the same one. If everything you need genuinely
has to run in the same room, the fix is a new dedicated circuit, not a bigger breaker.

Never fit a larger breaker to stop an overload trip. The breaker is sized to the wire
behind it. Fitting a 20-amp breaker on 14-gauge wire removes the protection and leaves
the wire to act as the fuse.

## It trips instantly, with nothing plugged in

This is a short circuit or a ground fault, and it is a different situation entirely.

The tell is that load makes no difference. You unplug everything on the circuit, reset the
breaker, and it trips immediately or within a second or two.

**What to do:** leave it off and call an electrician. That breaker is holding back a fault
somewhere in the fixed wiring — a damaged cable, a failed device, water where it shouldn't
be. Repeated resetting is the one genuinely dangerous response, because each reset dumps
fault current into whatever is broken.

If there is also a burning smell, a warm faceplate or scorching, treat it as urgent rather
than as a scheduling problem.

## It trips at random, and it has got worse over time

Breakers wear out. They are mechanical devices with a thermal element, and after enough
trips — or enough years running near capacity — they start opening below their rated
current.

The tell is history: the same circuit, the same load it has always carried, now trips in a
way it never used to, and the trips are getting more frequent.

**What to do:** this one is worth diagnosing rather than guessing. A worn breaker and an
early-stage loose connection look similar from the outside, and a loose connection makes
heat. Both are fixable; only one is urgent.

## What to note before you call

Whatever the cause, the same four details save real diagnostic time — which is your money,
since diagnosis is billed by the hour:

1. **What was running** when it tripped, including anything that cycles on by itself like
   a fridge, a freezer or a well pump.
2. **Whether it resets** and holds, resets and trips again shortly, or won't reset at all.
3. **Which breaker** it is, and what else goes dead when it opens.
4. **Anything you can smell, hear or feel** — a hum, a warm cover plate, a sharp smell.

That list is usually enough for us to arrive with the right parts rather than making two
trips.

## When it is not the breaker at all

Two cases get mistaken for breaker problems regularly.

A **tripped GFCI** upstream will kill several outlets at once while the breaker stays on.
Check bathrooms, the kitchen, the garage and outside walls for a receptacle with a reset
button before assuming the panel is involved.

An **AFCI breaker** — required on most living-area circuits in newer homes — trips on
arcing signatures rather than current. Certain motors and older power tools set them off
without anything being wrong. If the trips started when you began using a specific tool,
mention that.
